Several studies measured the traces of language models in the published record. Word frequencies suggest that most biomedical papers of 2025 had some model assistance ({{most-biomedical-publications-show-signs-of}}). The long decline of single-author papers stopped after 2022 ({{return-of-the-solo-author-the}}), and arXiv submissions outside computer science have run above trend since December 2025 ({{arxiv-apocalypse}}). An audit flagged likely fabricated references in accepted conference papers, with many false alarms ({{phantom-references-hallucinated-citations-that-s}}). Economics papers that disclose AI use credit more research assistants than their authors' other papers ({{ai-and-the-research-assistant}}).

Google and Anthropic each analyzed how scientists use their models ({{ai-in-science-early-insights}}, {{how-researchers-use-claude}}). Both find most use in data analysis, coding and writing, and the reported time savings are self-estimates. A randomized experiment at ICML found that a ban on language models in peer review did not change decisions and that many reviewers did not follow it ({{icml-2026-llm-peer-review}}). An analysis of published comparisons gives a base rate for how often AI methods beat traditional statistics ({{ai-enabled-scientific-frontier}}).

Two essays argue that experiments, validation and institutions, more than reasoning, limit what AI can add ({{why-ai-will-not-speed-up-science}}, {{when-scientific-cognition-is-no-longer-scarce}}).
