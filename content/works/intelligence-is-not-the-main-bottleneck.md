Teslo writes: "no matter how “intelligent” AI becomes, intelligence is often not the main bottleneck to things changing in the real world."

Her evidence comes mostly from drug development. The number of drugs approved per dollar of research spending has fallen for decades, "even as our scientific tools have grown vastly more powerful". Clinical trials, she writes, "consume something like seven years and cost more than a billion dollars per drug". AI could help validate surrogate endpoints, which would shorten trials, but there, she writes, "the binding constraint is not entirely intelligence. Quite often, it is governance." Her examples are companies waiting a year for access to public imaging data and a twelve-year process to validate one surrogate endpoint. She also argues that patent law "rewards novel chemical matter, not novel biology", which directs investment, including investment in AI, toward designing molecules for known targets.

The second half of the essay is about why people at AI companies discount these constraints. She describes parts of the San Francisco technology community as "something close to a monoculture" in which doubting that AI will be transformative carries a social cost. This part rests on her observation.

The essay relies on widely cited figures and published cases and presents no new data. In a footnote Teslo reports a reader's objection that it is unclear which of China's 2015 policy changes produced the growth of its biotechnology industry, which she uses as evidence for the value of faster trials.
