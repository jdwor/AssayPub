The essay assumes, without arguing for it, that AI becomes "better, faster, and cheaper than people" at nearly every scientific task that can be done at a computer: "Set aside whether this happens, or when." Nathan DeBardeleben of Los Alamos National Laboratory calls the result the scarcity inversion: "As machine reasoning becomes abundant, the scarce resources in science shift toward selecting worthwhile questions, obtaining contact with the physical world, determining which claims the evidence supports, and exercising accountable institutional authority."

Others have argued that experiment, more than ideas, limits science (for example {{intelligence-is-not-the-main-bottleneck}}). This essay takes mathematics and theoretical computer science as fields where the inversion is under way, because there the problem, the candidate answer and the check can all run on a computer. Its evidence is the year's record: the mathematical releases from OpenAI, including the October collection ({{openai-math-release}}), and a community table of the largest known graphs of given degree and diameter, which logged 192 record replacements in 2026 through October 7, in 80 of its 126 cells. The author notes that the table's history is incomplete and that the count "does not measure every advance or establish that AI caused the increase". Of the mathematical releases the essay says: "Candidate mathematical results are being produced at scale, while formalization, independent review, correction, and assimilation into the mathematical record become the limiting work."

On institutions, the essay raises the objection that selection and validation are themselves reasoning, so capable agents should make them cheap as well. Its reply is that some inputs lie outside computation. "An unmade measurement is unavailable to a model." Responsibility for a decision stays with a person or an institution: "Giving an agent operational control does not transfer that responsibility." From this the essay derives design advice for laboratories. Facility interfaces should state capabilities and costs in a form agents can use. A request for an experiment should say what uncertainty the measurement would resolve. Provenance should be recorded from an agent's first action. A review should stand between running an experiment and accepting its result, and some capacity should be kept for experiments that could overturn a program's assumptions. The essay also says that "model throughput is a poor measure of scientific capacity".

Much of the essay is about the national laboratories, the author's own employer. It argues that their facilities, controlled data and public authority gain in value, and concedes that a problem solved entirely by computation on public information "draws no advantage from a beamline, controlled material, or unique location". Several of its examples of agent systems are Los Alamos projects. Apart from the count from the graph table the essay presents no data of its own, and its premise is hypothetical throughout.
