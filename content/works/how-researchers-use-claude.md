Anthropic's economics team analyzed 207,049 sessions on consumer Claude plans from August 2 to September 30, 2026: 128,914 from the chat and Cowork interfaces and 78,135 from Claude Code. A language-model classifier decided whether each sampled session was research and assigned it a field and a task from SciNet, a taxonomy of 7,262 research tasks in 34 fields. Coursework and the work of students below doctoral level were excluded. The report also includes a survey of 528 scientists and a model of how AI could change the pace of research.

Computer scientists make up 4.3% of the world's researchers and 32.7% of research sessions in Claude Code. Data analysis accounts for 33.7% of sessions in the chat products and 48.1% in Claude Code; the report puts its share of researchers' time at 27.6%. Gathering data accounts for 11.5% and 11.9% of sessions, against 28.6% of researchers' time. Generating ideas and hypotheses accounts for 4.2% and 2.1%, against 7.1%.

The authors also score each research step for how well current AI could perform it. The score comes from a language model that was shown 317,560 pairs of steps and asked which of the two AI would do better. Tasks in the top fifth of the score receive about twice their expected share of use in chat and about four times in Claude Code. The report does not compare the score with measured performance on the tasks.

Most of the measurement rests on language models. SciNet's estimates of how researchers divide their time were generated by models and checked against time records in wet-lab protocols and the frequency of tasks in 31,500 open-access papers. The session classifier was tested on 5,000 synthetic transcripts written by a model, where it chose the right field in 83% of cases and the right task in 69%. The appendix says: "When no ground truth is available, validation of any classifier is inherently difficult."

The final section estimates how much faster research could go. With current capabilities and wide adoption, the authors calculate that AI could take on 27–52% of the researcher time needed for a new study, which implies speed-ups from 1.35x in the arts to 2.02x in statistics. These figures start from the survey. Respondents' reported levels of AI use are converted to shares of time saved, 25% for assistive use, 50% for shared use and so on, and applied to every field. In a scenario where AI performs every non-physical step, fields with little physical work reach about 15–16x and laboratory fields 3–4x. The ceiling follows from the assumed prices: an hour of AI costs $30 against $95 for a researcher, and AI runs for 0.2 hours for each researcher hour it saves. On the last figure the appendix says: "No data source measures AI running time by step". The report says of all the scenarios: "These are illustrative scenarios, not forecasts." It contains no measurement of research speed or output.

The session data cover consumer plans only, so enterprise and institutional use is missing. The survey went to 1,813 applicants to Anthropic's grant programs, and 528 completed it. The appendix says they are "drawn primarily from biological and medical sciences and lean toward computational research", and the report says they "are likely more intensive AI users than the typical scientist". The authors note that idea generation is hard to classify and may be undercounted.

Google's study of Gemini use ({{ai-in-science-early-insights}}) also finds that data analysis is the largest use, that computer science is overrepresented relative to its number of researchers, and that the health sciences are underrepresented.
