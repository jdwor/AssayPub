In August, *Nature Reviews Drug Discovery* published a review of the field by Andreas Bender and fifteen co-authors, and Derek Lowe used his post to summarize and endorse its argument. He calls it "an excellent new article from a number of people who know what they're talking about in the field and who (importantly) are not trying to sell you anything, either." The review's abstract states: "Although a wide variety of AI methods have been developed, applied and benchmarked, evidence of their clinically relevant impact is, so far, disappointingly limited". Lowe quotes it further: "It can be concluded that we are still at a stage with an 'absence of evidence' (and not necessarily an 'evidence of absence') when it comes to the translation of AI into clinically relevant impact."

His contribution is about where impact would have to appear. He writes: "The paper rightly identifies Phase II success rates as where you really would want to see the most impact from any purported improvements in drug development". "All that stuff in early-stage discovery work is a roundoff error in time and money compared to the clinical trials, and Phase II is where you first run into the really large cross-section for failure in the clinic." He adds that attribution is hard even without AI, because successful projects are credited to insight and failures are not discussed, so any technology applied across a portfolio will appear to have helped.

Lowe quotes the review on a problem he takes to be underappreciated: "Another key problem is that the nature of the data at hand in drug discovery — its confounding factors, conditionality and epistemic opacity — may not be understood by everyone aiming to model such data", with the consequence that benchmarks built on those labels measure the wrong thing. He is more skeptical than the review about whether the accumulated assay data can be cleaned up for training at all, writing that the field does not know how to categorize it and may not be able to.

He is less sure about the review's main recommendation, which he quotes as "the focus of AI in drug discovery must shift from doing what can be done - such as modelling data that is readily available, but that is unlikely to move the needle - to doing what should be done, even if this requires, for example, substantial data generation". Generating the right data is expensive and slow, and he expects most groups to wait for someone else to pay for it.

The post presents no new data; its authority rests on the review it reports and on the author's experience. The review is behind a paywall, and the post is the most widely read account of its argument.
