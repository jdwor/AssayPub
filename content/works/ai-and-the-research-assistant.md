Studies of AI in research usually observe a paper's authors. Research assistants, who clean data, write estimation code and assemble appendices, do work that language models now do, and economics working papers name them in their acknowledgements. Johan Fourie of Stellenbosch University collected 56,585 working papers from 23 institutional series and the economics section of arXiv, through October 2026, and recorded for each the assistants credited, whether the authors state that they used generative AI, and whether a detector flags the prose as resembling AI-generated text.

The comparison is within authors: papers after 2022 by the same economist, with and without each indicator. The main result is in the abstract: "Disclosing papers credit 0.548 more named assistants than other papers by the same economist, two-thirds of the mean, and run longer, mostly in their appendices." Both differences survive correction for the twenty tests the author registered. Differences in the number of authors and in whether any assistant is credited do not. The prose flag shows no consistent association with anything, and the two indicators barely overlap: about one disclosing paper in seven is flagged.

The paper then splits the disclosures by what the tool did. About four in five report coding, data work, analysis or derivation. "Most economists who disclose AI are reporting what it did to their data or their code, not to their prose." The whole assistant difference comes from those disclosures (0.679 more named assistants), and none of it from statements about writing alone. Fourie's reading is that cheaper execution leads researchers to widen a study, which can raise the demand for assistance even as each task takes fewer hours. In the institutional series the share of papers crediting any assistant stayed between 27% and 32% from 2022 to 2026 while disclosure rose from zero to about one paper in six.

Fourie writes that "The credits measure contributions to a paper, not hours or employment." Disclosure is voluntary: "A disclosure is a collective decision by an author team rather than an instrument reading of actual use." Papers that do not disclose may still have used AI. The variation comes from few places, since "the disclosure variation rests on about 3.3 effective sources", and the task split is exploratory, outside the registered tests. The author concludes that "unobserved project ambition and selective silence among non-disclosing papers remain part of every comparison".

The paper asks what this means for training: when a model takes the first pass at the routine work, "does checking still teach a novice how to do economics?" Its own acknowledgements say that "Anthropic’s Claude Fable coordinated the revision and edited the text, Google’s Gemini 4 Argon drafted the text".
