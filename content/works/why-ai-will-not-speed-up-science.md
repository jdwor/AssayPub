The essay responds to two papers on AI research systems published in Nature and to forecasts from heads of AI companies that the technology will compress decades of biology into years. Ran Blekhman grants that the systems do in hours what takes a team weeks of reading and analysis, and argues that this is not where the time goes.

"The actual bottlenecks in biomedical science are physical, institutional, and human." A grant takes 12 to 18 months from submission to award. Recruiting participants for a clinical study takes years. Cells have to be cultured, animals bred to the right genotype and sometimes aged, experiments repeated. Ethics and animal-use approvals, data agreements and material transfers each take months, and so does peer review. The essay applies Amdahl's law: "So if literature review and hypothesis generation account for 5% of a research project’s timeline, then even a 100X speedup in those tasks reduces the total project duration by less than 5%."

Its example is a startup that announced a laboratory able to go from AI-led drug design to experimental data in 24 hours. By the company's own account, one of the platform's six modules involves AI, and the speed came from an in-house animal facility, protocols approved in advance and staff on standby. Blekhman writes: "The speedup did not come from the AI, but from a vivarium, pre-approved protocols, veterinarians on standby, and wet-lab pipelines re-engineered to run faster."

The essay's concern is what others do with the forecasts. Administrators expect more output from fewer people, and "Funders hear that AI might offset the cuts to NIH budgets that are currently suffocating the research enterprise." It compares the present to the productivity paradox of the 1980s, when spending on computers took more than a decade to show up in measured productivity and did so only after organizations changed how they worked. The recommendation is addressed to institutions: "AI that analyzes a dataset 10X faster moves a project forward only marginally, but a proposal funded within 4 weeks of submission cuts years of wasted time."

The argument rests on experience, and its one number is hypothetical: the 5% share for literature review and hypothesis generation is introduced as a supposition, and no data on how research projects divide their time are offered. The survey it cites concerns business executives. The essay covers experimental biomedicine and does not consider fields where the work is done at a computer. A footnote adds that AI "lowers the cost of producing papers without lowering the cost of producing knowledge".
