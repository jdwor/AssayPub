The authors write that research agents raise "urgent questions for policymakers and science funders, the biggest being how to validate the coming wave of AI-generated ideas." They write: "AI agents are conjecture machines, making ideas and candidate solutions abundant and relatively cheap. Refutations remain physical and institutional — and so, costly and slow." They treat mathematics as the exception, because a proof can be checked by computer, and say that in most other fields the gap between proposing and testing is widening.

They describe what current models lack as "tacit knowledge: the hard-to-articulate craft, built up over years of practice and failure, that lets a scientist coax a cell line into growing or get a simulation to run well." They also quote a colleague on unreliability: "A single hallucinated claim on page 10 of an output can invalidate the whole thing".

The essay sets four priorities for governments and funders: give all scientists access to agents, make public datasets usable by agents, invest in experimental facilities and automated laboratories, and let peer reviewers use agents. It cites the US National Science Foundation's $100 million for a network of automated laboratories and names the Genesis Mission ({{genesis-mission-expansion}}) as a model for providing access. The authors also write: "AI agents must not be viewed as a rationale to spend less on science; this would be a tragic false economy."

Everyone interviewed works at Google DeepMind, and the systems used as examples, Co-Scientist, AlphaEvolve and Aletheia, are the company's own. One of the five authors led the Aletheia project. The essay appeared two months before a DeepMind paper on markets for experiments ({{agentic-economies}}), whose first author is among those thanked for interviews and comments.
