Daniel Litt gave this talk at a summit on the future of mathematics at OpenAI, where Sébastien Bubeck had asked for an account of the future to avoid. The summit took as its premise that AI will become reliably better than people at mathematics. Litt writes: "I want to tell a story in which, despite this, mathematical progress stalls." And then: "To be clear this is not a prediction".

The story starts from 2026 and from two charts. One shows combinatorics papers posted to arXiv each week rising steeply. The other shows questions and answers on MathOverflow falling: "What I find striking here is that there are both fewer questions and fewer answers". Litt adds cases of duplicated work. Three groups produced similar proofs of the same conjecture almost at once, and the AI companies have replicated one another's announced results. "The models, and the people using them, seem to be solving the same problems."

The profession rewards papers, theorems and resolved conjectures, and "these outputs are now mispriced". If the rewards stay as they are, "the dominant strategy for career success (at least in the medium term) is playing the slot machine for conjectures", with papers that no one reads, their authors included. Openness suffers as well, because a model can finish a problem from a few key ideas: "I've recently been told by multiple colleagues that they are unwilling to discuss work in progress for this reason." In the later years of the story, formalization becomes cheap and repairs errors in the literature, while mathematicians trust statements more than they understand ideas, and institutions that still count papers end up rewarding people who do not care about the mathematics.

Litt concludes: "These values persist in a world with highly capable AI, but the mechanisms we use to achieve them are in many cases not robust to highly capable AI." The post ends: "For what it's worth, I'm broadly optimistic that mathematics will survive and flourish."

The essay is a scenario written to order, and its author says correctness was not the priority. The two charts appear without sources or figures in the text, and the attribution of MathOverflow's decline to AI is the author's judgment. The rise in submissions was measured across arXiv two months later ({{arxiv-apocalypse}}).
